Child Health Conditions

Comprehensive clinical research on childhood conditions from AAP, RCPCH, WHO and NICE guidelines.

⚠️
All information is for educational research. Diagnosis requires clinical assessment by a qualified paediatrician. Children can deteriorate quickly — always seek medical advice if worried.

Quick Reference

Fever in Children

  • NICE traffic light system: green/amber/red risk stratification
  • ANY fever in infant under 3 months: urgent paediatric assessment
  • Most fevers are viral and self-limiting
  • Non-blanching rash + fever: EMERGENCY (meningococcal sepsis)

Bronchiolitis

  • Viral (RSV), affects infants under 12 months, peak 3-6 months
  • Self-limiting over 1-2 weeks; no role for antibiotics/steroids
  • Red flags: apnoea, SpO2 under 92%, poor feeding, exhaustion
  • Supportive care: oxygen if needed, feeding support

Vaccination

  • MMR-autism link definitively disproven (original study retracted)
  • Minor illness without fever is NOT a contraindication
  • Catch-up always possible — never too late, no restart needed
  • Serious side effects extremely rare; benefits far outweigh risks

Constipation in Children

  • Over 90% is functional (no organic cause)
  • Macrogol (Movicol Paediatric) first-line for disimpaction and maintenance
  • May take months for full resolution — do not stop laxatives too early
  • Red flags: delayed meconium, ribbon stools, failure to thrive

Vaccine Safety and Common Questions

Is the MMR vaccine linked to autism?

No. This has been extensively studied across millions of children worldwide. The original 1998 study suggesting a link was retracted by the journal and found to be fraudulent, and the author was struck off the medical register. No credible scientific evidence supports any link between vaccines and autism.

What side effects are normal?

Common and expected: soreness/redness at injection site, mild fever, fussiness or sleepiness for 1-2 days. These resolve on their own. Paracetamol can be used for discomfort if needed (check product age guidance).

Can my child be vaccinated if they have a cold?

Yes — minor illness without fever is NOT a reason to delay vaccination. Only moderate-severe acute illness with fever typically warrants a short delay, on your doctor's advice.

What if we missed some vaccines?

Catch-up is always possible at any age — it is never too late. There is no need to restart a vaccine series from the beginning, even after a long delay. Speak to your paediatrician or GP about a personalised catch-up schedule.

Who should NOT receive certain vaccines?

True contraindications are rare: anaphylaxis to a previous dose or vaccine component, and severe immunodeficiency for live vaccines (MMR, BCG, rotavirus, varicella). Your paediatrician will screen for these before each vaccination.

Paediatric Pharmacology

Children's Medicines

Research on commonly used paediatric medications from AAP, RCPCH, WHO and NICE guidelines.

⚠️
Medication dosing for children is weight-based and must be confirmed by a pharmacist or doctor. Never give adult medications or doses to children. Always use the dosing device provided with liquid medicines.

Pain Relief and Fever Medications

  • Paracetamol: 15mg/kg every 4-6 hours (maximum 4 doses/24 hours). Available as liquid suspension (age/weight-banded dosing on packaging), suppositories, tablets for older children. First-line for pain and fever discomfort in children over 2 months (weight-based dosing essential under medical guidance for infants under 3 months).
  • Ibuprofen: 5-10mg/kg every 6-8 hours (maximum 3 doses/24 hours). Avoid in: dehydration, chickenpox (theoretical risk of severe skin/soft tissue infection), asthma with known NSAID sensitivity, under 3 months (unless directed by doctor). Can alternate with paracetamol if fever persists and one alone is insufficient, on medical advice.
  • Important: Antipyretics are for comfort, not solely to reduce a number on the thermometer. Do not use to try to prevent febrile seizures — no evidence this works. Always use the measuring device (syringe/spoon) provided, not a kitchen spoon.
  • Aspirin: AVOID in children under 16 years (except specific conditions under specialist guidance) — risk of Reye syndrome, particularly with viral illness (e.g. chickenpox, flu).

Antibiotics in Children

  • Amoxicillin: First-line for many common bacterial infections (otitis media, sinusitis, some pneumonia, UTI in some areas). Typical dosing 20-90mg/kg/day in divided doses depending on indication and severity — exact dose determined by treating doctor.
  • Co-amoxiclav (Augmentin): Broader spectrum, used when amoxicillin resistance suspected or for specific infections (e.g. animal bites, some respiratory infections).
  • Azithromycin/Clarithromycin (macrolides): Alternative for penicillin-allergic children, used for atypical pneumonia, pertussis (whooping cough).
  • Important principles: Most childhood infections are viral and do NOT need antibiotics (colds, most coughs, most sore throats, bronchiolitis). Antibiotics should only be used when a bacterial infection is identified or strongly suspected by a clinician. Always complete the full course as prescribed unless advised otherwise. Antibiotic resistance is a major global health concern — appropriate use protects future effectiveness.

Asthma and Wheeze Inhalers

  • Salbutamol (reliever/blue inhaler): Short-acting beta-2 agonist. Used for acute symptom relief during wheeze/breathlessness. Via spacer device for young children (improves drug delivery, reduces side effects) — technique matters more than the device itself.
  • Inhaled corticosteroids (preventer/brown inhaler): Beclometasone, fluticasone, budesonide. Taken regularly (not just during symptoms) to reduce airway inflammation and prevent attacks. Rinse mouth after use to reduce oral thrush risk. Takes days-weeks to show full benefit.
  • Spacer devices: Essential for effective inhaler use in young children — dramatically improves lung deposition of medication versus inhaler alone. Different sizes/types for different ages (with mask for infants/toddlers, mouthpiece for older children).
  • Personalised Asthma Action Plan: Every child with asthma should have one — outlines daily management and what to do if symptoms worsen, including when to seek emergency care.

Allergy and Skin Medications

  • Antihistamines: Cetirizine, loratadine (non-drowsy, once-daily, age-appropriate liquid formulations available) for allergic symptoms (hay fever, hives, itching). Chlorphenamine (Piriton) is sedating — sometimes used short-term for itch/sleep disturbance during eczema or chickenpox flares, but newer non-sedating options often preferred.
  • Emollients: The mainstay of eczema management. Apply liberally and frequently (several times daily), even when skin appears clear, to maintain skin barrier function. Many types available (creams, ointments, lotions) — finding one the child tolerates well improves adherence.
  • Topical corticosteroids: For eczema flares, potency matched to severity and body site (milder potency for face/skin folds, stronger for thick skin like palms/soles if needed). Apply to affected areas during flares as directed, then step down as skin improves. Fear of steroids (steroid phobia) often leads to under-treatment — appropriate use under guidance is safe and effective.
  • Adrenaline auto-injectors (EpiPen/Jext): For children with diagnosed severe allergy at risk of anaphylaxis. Carried at all times, used at first signs of severe reaction (difficulty breathing, throat/facial swelling, widespread hives with systemic symptoms). All carers and schools should be trained in use.

Vitamins and Supplements

  • Vitamin D: 400 IU/day recommended for breastfed infants from birth (formula is fortified). Often recommended for all children, especially with limited sun exposure or darker skin tone, per national guidance.
  • Multivitamin drops (A, C, D): Recommended in some countries (e.g. UK Healthy Start vitamins) for children with limited dietary variety, typically from 6 months to 5 years.
  • Iron: Term infants generally have adequate iron stores until around 6 months from maternal transfer. Preterm or low birth weight infants often need earlier supplementation — discuss with paediatrician. Iron-rich complementary foods important from 6 months for all infants.
  • Probiotics: Some evidence for use in acute gastroenteritis (may modestly reduce duration) and antibiotic-associated diarrhoea prevention. Evidence less robust for other indications (colic, eczema prevention) — discuss with paediatrician before routine use.
URGENT

Paediatric Emergency Guide

🚨
CHILD EMERGENCIES — CALL 112 (India) / 999 (UK) / 911 (US) IMMEDIATELY: Difficulty breathing, blue lips/face, seizure, unresponsive/floppy child, non-blanching rash with fever, severe dehydration, anaphylaxis, choking that does not resolve. These are time-critical emergencies.

🚨 Breathing Difficulty

Recognition: Grunting, severe chest indrawing, nasal flaring, RR over 60 (any age), unable to feed/talk due to breathlessness, blue lips/tongue, exhaustion, SpO2 under 92%, silent chest (in known asthma — very serious).

Immediate Actions

  • Call 112/999/911 immediately
  • Keep child upright/comfortable — do NOT force lying flat
  • If known asthmatic with inhaler available, give as per action plan while awaiting help
  • Stay calm — anxiety can worsen breathing difficulty in the child

🚨 Choking

Infant under 1 year (conscious): 5 back blows (infant face-down along your forearm, head lower than chest) then 5 chest thrusts (infant face-up, two fingers on lower breastbone) if back blows fail. Repeat cycle.

Child over 1 year (conscious): 5 back blows then 5 abdominal thrusts (Heimlich manoeuvre) if back blows fail. Repeat cycle.

Critical Points

  • If child becomes unconscious: start CPR immediately, check mouth for visible object before each set of rescue breaths
  • Call 112/999/911 if choking does not resolve quickly or child loses consciousness

🚨 Seizures

First aid for any seizure: Time it. Protect from injury (clear hazards, cushion head). Do NOT restrain or put anything in the mouth. Once jerking stops, place in recovery position.

Call Emergency Services If

  • First-ever seizure
  • Lasts longer than 5 minutes
  • Difficulty breathing afterwards, or does not regain consciousness
  • Injury occurs during the seizure
  • Known epileptic but this seizure differs from their usual pattern
  • Another seizure starts before recovery from the first

🚨 Anaphylaxis (Severe Allergic Reaction)

Recognition: Difficulty breathing/wheeze, swelling of lips/face/throat, widespread hives, dizziness/collapse, vomiting — in the context of allergen exposure.

Immediate Actions

  • Call 112/999/911
  • Use adrenaline auto-injector (EpiPen/Jext) immediately if prescribed and available — inject into outer thigh
  • Lay child flat with legs raised (or sitting if breathing difficulty predominates), unless vomiting (use recovery position)
  • Second adrenaline dose after 5-15 minutes if no improvement before help arrives

Other Paediatric Emergencies

  • Non-blanching rash with fever: Press a glass against the rash — if it does NOT fade, this is a medical emergency (possible meningococcal sepsis) regardless of how the child otherwise appears. Call 112/999/911 immediately.
  • Severe dehydration: Lethargic/unconscious, sunken eyes, very dry mouth, very slow skin pinch recoil, no urine output, sunken fontanelle (infants). Medical emergency requiring urgent fluid resuscitation.
  • Head injury red flags: Loss of consciousness, repeated vomiting, abnormal drowsiness, fluid from nose/ears, seizure, any swelling/bruise in infant under 1 year — seek urgent assessment.
  • Burns/scalds: Cool under running water for 20 minutes immediately. Seek emergency care for burns to face/hands/feet/genitals, large burns, or any burn in an infant under 1 year.
  • Safeguarding concerns: If you are worried about a child's safety or suspect non-accidental injury, contact your local child protection services, GP, or in the UK call the NSPCC helpline. In an emergency, call 112/999/911.

Newborn Care

Research on newborn health, common concerns, and routine checks from RCPCH and WHO guidance.

Routine Newborn Checks

  • Newborn and Infant Physical Examination (NIPE): Within 72 hours of birth, repeated at 6-8 weeks. Checks eyes (red reflex), heart (murmurs, femoral pulses), hips (developmental dysplasia screening), and testes (descent) in boys.
  • Newborn blood spot screening (heel prick): Day 5, screens for congenital hypothyroidism, phenylketonuria (PKU), cystic fibrosis, sickle cell disease, and other inherited metabolic conditions per national programme.
  • Newborn hearing screening: OAE (otoacoustic emissions) test before discharge or by 4-5 weeks.

Jaundice

Affects up to 60% of term and 80% of preterm babies. Physiological jaundice: appears day 2-3, peaks day 3-5, resolves by 2 weeks (term) or 3 weeks (preterm) — due to immature liver function plus normal red cell breakdown.

Pathological jaundice requiring urgent assessment: onset within 24 hours of birth, prolonged beyond 2-3 weeks, very high bilirubin levels, or accompanied by poor feeding, lethargy, pale stools, or dark urine.

Treatment: Phototherapy for significant hyperbilirubinaemia based on NICE threshold charts (by age in hours and risk factors). Exchange transfusion reserved for severe cases.

Feeding

  • Exclusive breastfeeding recommended for first 6 months (WHO/UNICEF), continuing alongside complementary foods to 2 years or beyond.
  • Formula feeding is an appropriate alternative when breastfeeding is not possible or chosen — prepare strictly per manufacturer instructions, use paced feeding to avoid overfeeding.
  • Early hunger cues: rooting, hand-to-mouth movements, lip-smacking. Crying is a LATE hunger cue — try to feed before this point.
  • Normal weight loss: up to 10% of birth weight in the first 3-5 days is expected (fluid shifts plus establishing feeding). Should regain birth weight by 2-3 weeks — if not, feeding assessment needed.

Umbilical Cord Care

The cord stump dries and separates naturally within 5-15 days. Keep clean and dry, fold the nappy below the cord to avoid contact. Signs of infection (omphalitis) requiring urgent review: redness spreading from the cord base, pus, foul smell, or fever — this can progress rapidly in newborns.

Paediatric Nutrition

Child Nutrition

Evidence-based nutrition guidance for infants, toddlers and children from WHO and national paediatric guidelines.

Introducing Solid Foods (Weaning)

Complementary feeding typically starts around 6 months (not before 4 months), alongside continued breastfeeding or formula. Signs of readiness: can sit with support, good head control, can bring objects to the mouth, shows interest in food.

  • Start with single-ingredient purees or soft finger foods (baby-led weaning approach)
  • Introduce allergenic foods early and one at a time (egg, peanut, dairy) — current evidence (LEAP study) suggests early introduction may reduce allergy risk rather than increase it
  • Avoid added salt and sugar in the first year; avoid honey before 12 months (botulism risk)
  • Offer a wide variety of tastes and textures to support acceptance of diverse foods later

Picky Eating

Extremely common in toddlers and usually a normal developmental phase. Strategies: offer variety without pressure, model eating behaviour as a family, involve the child in food preparation, repeated exposure without force-feeding (may take 10-15 exposures before a new food is accepted), avoid using food as reward or punishment, maintain a regular meal and snack routine.

Seek advice if: very limited food range is affecting growth, extreme distress around food or mealtimes, or signs of nutritional deficiency — a feeding therapy referral may help.

Childhood Obesity

Defined as BMI above the 95th centile for age and sex on growth charts. Multifactorial causes: dietary patterns, physical activity levels, sleep, family and environmental factors, occasionally endocrine or genetic causes.

Management is a whole-family lifestyle approach rather than singling out the child: increase physical activity, reduce screen time, improve dietary quality (reduce sugary drinks and processed food, increase fruit and vegetables), and address any psychological factors. Avoid restrictive dieting language with children — focus on healthy habits for the whole family.

Food Allergy

True food allergy affects 6-8% of children, most commonly to cow's milk, egg, peanut, tree nuts, soy, wheat, fish, and shellfish. IgE-mediated reactions occur rapidly (minutes to 2 hours): hives, swelling, vomiting, or anaphylaxis.

Management: strict avoidance of the confirmed allergen, a written allergy action plan, and regular specialist review — many children outgrow milk and egg allergy, while peanut and tree nut allergy more often persist into adulthood. Avoid unnecessary exclusion diets without a confirmed diagnosis, as this risks nutritional deficiency and may delay natural tolerance development.

When to Seek Assessment

  • No eye contact by 3 months, no smiling by 8 weeks
  • Head circumference crossing centiles
  • Not sitting by 12 months, not walking by 18 months
  • No words by 18 months, no 2-word phrases by 2 years
  • Regression in any previously acquired skill, at any age
  • Persistent toe-walking, asymmetric movements

Autism Spectrum Disorder

A neurodevelopmental condition affecting social communication and behaviour, presenting in early childhood. Core features: differences in social communication and interaction, restricted or repetitive behaviours and interests, and sensory sensitivities.

Early signs may include reduced eye contact, delayed language, reduced response to name, repetitive movements, intense focus on specific interests, and difficulty with changes in routine. Diagnosis requires comprehensive multidisciplinary assessment — there is no single test. Early intervention (speech therapy, occupational therapy, behavioural approaches) improves outcomes, so earlier identification allows earlier support.

ADHD

A persistent pattern of inattention and/or hyperactivity-impulsivity affecting function across settings (home, school), present before age 12. Assessment requires detailed history from multiple settings (parents and teachers) and ruling out other causes.

Management: psychoeducation and parent training first-line for under-5s. For school-age children: behavioural interventions, and medication (methylphenidate first-line) if significant impairment persists despite environmental adaptations — always under specialist initiation and monitoring.

Speech and Language Delay

Has many possible causes: hearing impairment (always check first), bilingual exposure (normal, not a cause for delay on its own), autism spectrum, global developmental delay, specific language impairment, or environmental factors. A hearing test is essential for any child with speech delay, alongside speech and language therapy assessment.
